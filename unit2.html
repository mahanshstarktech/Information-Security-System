Security Attacks — Active vs Passive

🔥 92% — Every Paper Part A 🔮 2026 Predicted

What is a Security Attack?

A security attack is any action that compromises the security of information or the information systems of an organization. Attacks can target confidentiality, integrity, availability, or authentication of data and systems.

A security attack is anything someone does to try to break into your data, steal it, change it, or stop you from accessing it. Think of it as different ways a burglar might try to break into your house — they might just peek through the window (passive) or actually break in and rearrange furniture (active).

Attacks are broadly classified into two categories based on whether they modify the data being transmitted:

Passive Attacks

A passive attack involves monitoring or eavesdropping on transmissions. The attacker does NOT modify data — they only observe and collect information.

A passive attacker is like someone secretly reading your diary. They don't change anything — they just read. You wouldn't even know they were there because nothing looks different.

Two types of Passive Attacks:

  • Release of message contents: Reading the content of transmitted messages — e.g., reading an unencrypted email containing sensitive data
  • Traffic analysis: Even if messages are encrypted, attackers observe patterns — who communicates with whom, how often, at what times, and message lengths. This can reveal sensitive information without breaking encryption.

Key properties: Hard to detect (leaves no trace) · Prevention: Encryption · Target: Confidentiality

Active Attacks

An active attack involves modifying, replaying, or creating data. The attacker actively interferes with the communication.

An active attacker is like a burglar who actually breaks into your house and rearranges furniture (or steals things, or leaves fake notes). They interact with your stuff — they don't just watch.

Four types of Active Attacks:

  • Masquerade: Pretending to be someone else. Attacker impersonates a legitimate entity — e.g., sending an email pretending to be your bank manager.
  • Replay: Capturing a valid transmission and re-sending it later. Example: capturing a bank's authentication message and replaying it to get unauthorized access.
  • Modification of messages: Altering, delaying, or reordering messages. Example: changing "Transfer ₹100 to Account A" to "Transfer ₹1,00,000 to Account B".
  • Denial of Service (DoS): Preventing legitimate access to resources by overwhelming the system. Example: flooding a web server with millions of fake requests until it crashes.

Key properties: Easier to detect (causes disruption) · Prevention: Authentication + Integrity checks (MACs, digital signatures) · Target: Integrity, Availability, Authenticity

Active vs Passive — Key Distinction (2-mark format)
Passive: Eavesdrop only. No modification. Goal = Confidentiality breach.
Prevention = Encryption. Detection = Very difficult.
Active: Modifies/creates/replays data. Goal = Integrity/Availability/Auth breach.
Prevention = Authentication + MAC + Digital Signatures. Detection = Easier.

Substitution Ciphers:
A substitution cipher replaces each letter/group of letters in the plaintext with another letter/group according to a fixed rule. The positions of letters change but the letters themselves are replaced. Examples: Caesar, Playfair, Hill, Vigenère.

Transposition Ciphers:
A transposition cipher rearranges the letters/characters of the plaintext without changing the letters themselves. The letters stay the same but their positions change. Example: Rail Fence, columnar transposition.

Key difference: Substitution = What letters (identity changes); Transposition = Where letters go (position changes).

Security Services & Mechanisms

Security Services (What we want to protect)

Defined by ITU-T X.800. A security service is a capability that protects against a specific type of attack:

ServiceWhat it providesThreatened by
AuthenticationAssurance of identity of communicating entityMasquerade attack
Access ControlPrevents unauthorized use of resourcesPrivilege escalation
Data ConfidentialityProtection from unauthorized disclosurePassive attacks (eavesdropping)
Data IntegrityAssurance data has not been alteredModification attacks
Non-RepudiationSender/receiver cannot deny a messageDenial of sending/receiving
AvailabilitySystem accessible when neededDenial of Service

Security Mechanisms (How we achieve services)

  • Encipherment: Transformation of data using algorithms (DES, AES, RSA) — provides confidentiality
  • Digital Signature: Data that proves authenticity and provides non-repudiation
  • Access Control: Rights, permissions, capabilities management
  • Data Integrity: Hash functions to detect modification
  • Authentication Exchange: Protocols to verify identity (passwords, Kerberos)
  • Traffic Padding: Inserting dummy bits to defeat traffic analysis
  • Routing Control: Selecting physically secure routes for sensitive traffic
  • Notarization: Trusted third party records communications for non-repudiation

Caesar Cipher

60% · Part A

What is the Caesar Cipher?

The Caesar cipher (also called ROT-3 or shift cipher) is the simplest and earliest known substitution cipher. Each letter of the plaintext is shifted by a fixed number of positions (key k) in the alphabet. Named after Julius Caesar who used k=3.

Imagine the alphabet written in a circle. To encrypt, you move each letter 3 steps forward. "A" becomes "D", "B" becomes "E", "Z" wraps around and becomes "C". To decrypt, move 3 steps back. It's so simple that even a child can break it by trying all 25 possible shifts.
Caesar Cipher Formula
Encryption: C = (P + k) mod 26
Decryption: P = (C - k + 26) mod 26
where: P = plaintext letter (0=A, 1=B, ..., 25=Z), k = key (shift amount)
Caesar used k=3. Key space = only 25 possible keys → easily brute-forced!
📌 Caesar Cipher Example (k=3)
Plaintext:  H E L L O   W O R L D
            7 4 11 11 14  22 14 17 11 3

Add k=3:    10 7 14 14 17  25 17 20 14 6

Ciphertext: K H O O R   Z R U O G

Decryption: K(10)-3=H(7)=H, H(7)-3=E(4)=E... → HELLO WORLD ✓

Weaknesses of Caesar Cipher

  • Tiny key space: Only 25 possible keys (k=1 to 25) — attacker can try ALL keys in seconds
  • Frequency analysis: Letter frequency preserved — 'E' (most common in English) → same ciphertext letter always
  • No key management: Single number as key is trivial to guess

Playfair Cipher

88% · Part B 🔮 2026 Predicted
🔮 2026 HIGH Confidence

Playfair appeared in 2024 Part B Q1 (key="occurrence", message="Jaipur") and 2025 Part B Q1 (key="Code", with Hill Cipher). Playfair alternates with Hill Cipher in Part B. Since Hill Cipher is predicted strongly for 2026, Playfair may appear as the alternative Part B question.

What is the Playfair Cipher?

The Playfair cipher is a digraph (letter pair) substitution cipher invented by Charles Wheatstone in 1854, popularized by Lord Playfair. Instead of encrypting one letter at a time, it encrypts pairs of letters using a 5×5 key matrix. This defeats simple frequency analysis since it analyzes pairs, not individual letters.

Instead of replacing one letter at a time (like Caesar), Playfair replaces TWO letters at a time using a secret 5×5 grid. You build the grid from a keyword, then look up pairs of letters in the grid and apply rules to encrypt them. It's like a more complex version of "find the letters in this word search grid and follow these directions."

Step 1 — Build the 5×5 Matrix

Rules for building the Playfair matrix:

1
Write the keyword, removing duplicate letters: Key="OCCURRENCE" → remove duplicates: O-C-U-R-E-N
2
Fill remaining alphabet letters (A-Z, combining I and J as one cell) after removing keyword letters
3
Fill the 5×5 grid row by row with keyword letters first, then remaining letters
📌 Playfair Matrix — Key = "OCCURRENCE"
Key = OCCURRENCE → Remove duplicates → O C U R E N (unique letters in order)
Remaining (unused) letters: A B D F G H I/J K L M P Q S T V W X Y Z

5×5 Playfair Matrix:
  ┌───┬───┬───┬───┬───┐
  │ O │ C │ U │ R │ E │   ← keyword letters first
  ├───┼───┼───┼───┼───┤
  │ N │ A │ B │ D │ F │   ← remaining letters
  ├───┼───┼───┼───┼───┤
  │ G │ H │ I │ K │ L │   (I and J share one cell)
  ├───┼───┼───┼───┼───┤
  │ M │ P │ Q │ S │ T │
  ├───┼───┼───┼───┼───┤
  │ V │ W │ X │ Y │ Z │
  └───┴───┴───┴───┴───┘

Step 2 — Prepare the Plaintext

  • Split plaintext into digraphs (pairs of 2 letters)
  • If both letters in a pair are same, insert 'X' between them: "LL" → "LX L"
  • If odd number of letters, add 'X' at the end
  • Replace J with I throughout
📌 Preparing "JAIPUR" for Playfair
Plaintext: JAIPUR → replace J with I → IAIPUR
Split into pairs: IA  IP  UR
No duplicate pairs, no odd ending → 3 digraphs: IA, IP, UR

Step 3 — Encryption Rules (3 Rules)

For each digraph (pair), apply the FIRST matching rule:

Playfair Encryption Rules
Rule 1 — SAME ROW: Replace each letter with the letter to its RIGHT (wrap around)
Rule 2 — SAME COLUMN: Replace each letter with the letter BELOW it (wrap around)
Rule 3 — RECTANGLE: Each letter replaced by letter in same row but the OTHER letter's column
For decryption: Rule 1 → go LEFT; Rule 2 → go UP; Rule 3 → same rectangle rule (symmetric)
📌 Encrypting "JAIPUR" with key "OCCURRENCE"
Matrix:    O C U R E
           N A B D F
           G H I K L
           M P Q S T
           V W X Y Z

Digraph 1: I A
  I is at row 2, col 2 (0-indexed)
  A is at row 1, col 1
  → Different row AND different column → RECTANGLE Rule
  → I's row=2, A's column=1: M[2][1] = H
  → A's row=1, I's column=2: M[1][2] = B
  → IA → HB ✓

Digraph 2: I P
  I is at row 2, col 2
  P is at row 3, col 1
  → RECTANGLE Rule
  → I's row=2, P's col=1: M[2][1] = H
  → P's row=3, I's col=2: M[3][2] = Q
  → IP → HQ ✓

Digraph 3: U R
  U is at row 0, col 2
  R is at row 0, col 3
  → SAME ROW Rule → move right
  → U→R (one right), R→E (one right)
  → UR → RE ✓

Ciphertext: HB HQ RE → HBHQRE
💡
Playfair Memory Trick — "RSR Rule"

Row → go Right (encryption) / Left (decryption). Same column → go Down / Up. Rectangle → swap columns but keep rows. Always check Row first, then Column, then Rectangle. For exam: write the matrix clearly first — it earns marks even if you make a calculation error later.

✅
Answer Validation Trick

To verify your Playfair encryption: decrypt your ciphertext using the REVERSE rules (Left instead of Right, Up instead of Down, same Rectangle). If you get back the original plaintext, your encryption is correct. Also count: ciphertext should have the same number of digraphs as plaintext.

Hill Cipher — Matrix Encryption

🔥 95% — Almost Every Paper! 🔮 2026 Predicted
🔮 2026 CERTAIN — Appears Every Year

Hill Cipher numerical appeared in 2023-Jul Part B Q1 (key [[3,2],[8,5]], message="Code") and 2025 Part B Q1 (same key [[3,2],[8,5]]). RTU uses the same key matrix repeatedly with different messages. For 2026, expect: same key [[3,2],[8,5]] OR [[1,2],[8,5]] with a new 4-letter message. Practice Hill Cipher until you can do it in your sleep.

What is the Hill Cipher?

The Hill cipher (1929, by Lester Hill) is a polygraphic substitution cipher based on linear algebra. It encrypts blocks of letters simultaneously using matrix multiplication. A 2×2 key matrix encrypts 2 letters at a time; a 3×3 key matrix encrypts 3 letters at a time.

The Hill cipher treats your message as a column of numbers (A=0, B=1, ..., Z=25) and multiplies it by a secret matrix (the key) using matrix multiplication, then takes the result modulo 26. It's like a scrambler that mixes multiple letters together at once — changing one input letter changes multiple output letters, making it harder to break than Caesar or even Playfair.

Hill Cipher — Encryption (Step by Step)

Hill Cipher Formula
Encryption: C = K × P (mod 26)
Decryption: P = K⁻¹ × C (mod 26)
where: K = n×n key matrix, P = plaintext column vector (n letters), K⁻¹ = modular inverse of K
All arithmetic is modulo 26. Letters: A=0, B=1, C=2, D=3, E=4, ..., Z=25
📌 Complete Example — Encrypt "CODE" with K = [[3,2],[8,5]]
Key matrix: K = [ 3  2 ]
                [ 8  5 ]

Convert "CODE" to numbers:
C=2, O=14, D=3, E=4

Split into 2-letter blocks: [C,O] and [D,E]

Block 1: Encrypt [C,O] = [2, 14]ᵀ

C = K × P (mod 26)
[ 3  2 ] × [ 2  ] = [ 3×2 + 2×14 ] = [ 6 + 28  ] = [ 34 ] ≡ [ 8  ] (mod 26)
[ 8  5 ]   [ 14 ]   [ 8×2 + 5×14 ]   [ 16 + 70 ]   [ 86 ]   [ 8  ]

8 = I, 8 = I → Block 1 encrypts to "II"

Block 2: Encrypt [D,E] = [3, 4]ᵀ

[ 3  2 ] × [ 3 ] = [ 3×3 + 2×4 ] = [ 9 + 8  ] = [ 17 ] ≡ [ 17 ] (mod 26)
[ 8  5 ]   [ 4 ]   [ 8×3 + 5×4 ]   [ 24 + 20]   [ 44 ]   [ 18 ]

17 = R, 18 = S → Block 2 encrypts to "RS"

Final Ciphertext: IIRS ✓

Hill Cipher — Decryption

Decryption requires the modular inverse matrix K⁻¹. To find K⁻¹ (mod 26) for a 2×2 matrix:

Finding K⁻¹ for K = [[a,b],[c,d]]
det(K) = ad - bc
K⁻¹ = det(K)⁻¹ × adj(K) (mod 26)
adj(K) = [ d -b ] (swap a,d; negate b,c)
[ -c a ]
det(K)⁻¹ = modular multiplicative inverse of det(K) modulo 26
det⁻¹ exists only if gcd(det(K), 26) = 1. Find x such that det(K)×x ≡ 1 (mod 26)
📌 Decrypt "IIRS" with K = [[3,2],[8,5]]
K = [ 3  2 ]
    [ 8  5 ]

Step 1: Find det(K)
det(K) = 3×5 - 2×8 = 15 - 16 = -1 ≡ 25 (mod 26)

Step 2: Find det(K)⁻¹ (mod 26)
We need x such that 25x ≡ 1 (mod 26)
25 × 25 = 625 = 24×26 + 1 → 625 ≡ 1 (mod 26)
So det(K)⁻¹ = 25

Step 3: Find adj(K)
adj(K) = [  5  -2 ] = [  5  24 ] (mod 26, since -2 ≡ 24)
         [ -8   3 ]   [ 18   3 ]   (since -8 ≡ 18 mod 26)

Step 4: K⁻¹ = det⁻¹ × adj(K) (mod 26)
K⁻¹ = 25 × [  5  24 ] = [ 125  600 ] ≡ [  21  2 ] (mod 26)
            [ 18   3 ]   [ 450   75 ]   [   8  23]

Check: 125 mod 26 = 125 - 4×26 = 125-104 = 21 ✓
       600 mod 26 = 600 - 23×26 = 600-598 = 2 ✓
       450 mod 26 = 450 - 17×26 = 450-442 = 8 ✓
       75 mod 26 = 75 - 2×26 = 75-52 = 23 ✓

K⁻¹ = [ 21   2 ]
      [  8  23 ]

Step 5: Decrypt Block 1: [I,I] = [8,8]ᵀ
P = K⁻¹ × C = [ 21  2 ] × [ 8 ] = [ 21×8 + 2×8 ] = [ 168+16 ] = [ 184 ] ≡ [ 2  ] (mod 26)
               [  8 23 ]   [ 8 ]   [  8×8 +23×8 ]   [  64+184]   [ 248 ]   [ 14 ]

2=C, 14=O → "CO" ✓

Step 6: Decrypt Block 2: [R,S] = [17,18]ᵀ
P = [ 21   2 ] × [ 17 ] = [ 21×17 + 2×18 ] = [ 357+36 ] = [ 393 ] ≡ [ 3  ] (mod 26)
    [  8  23 ]   [ 18 ]   [  8×17 +23×18 ]   [ 136+414]   [ 550 ]   [ 4  ]

3=D, 4=E → "DE" ✓

Decrypted: CODE ✓ (Original plaintext recovered!)
⚡
Hill Cipher Exam Speed Tricks

1. Letter-to-number cheatsheet: A=0,B=1,C=2,D=3,E=4,...,I=8,...,O=14,...,R=17,S=18,...,Z=25. Write this at the start.
2. Modulo shortcut: 26+n (if n is negative), or subtract 26s from large numbers. 86 mod 26 = 86-78 = 8 (since 3×26=78).
3. Validation: Decrypt your ciphertext using K⁻¹. If you get back plaintext, you're correct. This takes 2 mins but guarantees full marks.
4. If det(K)=-1: det⁻¹ = 25 (since 25×25=625≡1 mod26). This is the most common case in RTU papers!

Vigenère Cipher

70% · Part A/B

What is the Vigenère Cipher?

The Vigenère cipher is a polyalphabetic substitution cipher that uses multiple Caesar ciphers with different shift values, determined by a repeating keyword. It was considered "le chiffre indéchiffrable" (the unbreakable cipher) for 300 years — until Kasiski broke it in 1863.

Instead of shifting all letters by the same amount (like Caesar), the Vigenère cipher shifts by different amounts based on a keyword. If your keyword is "KEY", the first letter shifts by K=10, the second by E=4, the third by Y=24, and then the pattern repeats. It's like using multiple Caesar ciphers in rotation.
Vigenère Cipher Formula
Encryption: Cᵢ = (Pᵢ + Kᵢ) mod 26
Decryption: Pᵢ = (Cᵢ - Kᵢ + 26) mod 26
where: Pᵢ = plaintext letter i, Kᵢ = keyword letter i (cycling), Cᵢ = ciphertext letter i
📌 Vigenère Cipher — Encrypt "ATTACK" with key "KEY"
Plaintext:   A  T  T  A  C  K
             0  19 19  0  2  10

Key:         K  E  Y  K  E  Y  (repeat as needed)
             10  4 24 10  4  24

C = (P+K) mod 26:
A+K = 0+10 = 10 = K
T+E = 19+4 = 23 = X
T+Y = 19+24 = 43 mod 26 = 17 = R
A+K = 0+10 = 10 = K
C+E = 2+4 = 6 = G
K+Y = 10+24 = 34 mod 26 = 8 = I

Ciphertext: KXRKGI

Decryption (verify):
K-K = 10-10 = 0 = A ✓
X-E = 23-4 = 19 = T ✓
R-Y = 17-24 = -7 → -7+26 = 19 = T ✓
... → ATTACK ✓

Why is Vigenère Stronger than Caesar?

  • The same plaintext letter encrypts to DIFFERENT ciphertext letters depending on its position
  • Key space = 26ⁿ where n = key length (much larger than Caesar's 25)
  • Defeats simple frequency analysis — 'E' doesn't always map to the same ciphertext letter
  • Weakness: Kasiski test can find key length by detecting repeated patterns in ciphertext

Transposition Ciphers

82% · Part B 🔮 2026 Predicted

What is a Transposition Cipher?

A transposition cipher rearranges (permutes) the positions of the characters in the plaintext WITHOUT changing the characters themselves. The letters stay the same — only their positions change.

A transposition cipher is like cutting up a sentence written on paper, putting all the words in a bag, shaking it up, then gluing them down in a different order. You can still see the same words — they're just in the wrong places. The secret is knowing what order they should have been in.

Key difference from Substitution: Substitution = WHAT letters (letters change, positions preserved). Transposition = WHERE letters go (letters preserved, positions change).

Rail Fence Cipher

The plaintext is written diagonally across a number of "rails" (rows), then read off row by row to produce the ciphertext.

📌 Rail Fence — "INFORMATION SECURITY" with 3 rails
Plaintext: INFORMATIONSECURITY (spaces removed)

Write diagonally across 3 rails:
Rail 1:  I . . . M . . . I . . . S . . . R . . 
Rail 2:  . N . O . A . I . N . E . U . I . Y .
Rail 3:  . . F . . . T . . . O . . . C . . . .

Rail 1: I M I S R
Rail 2: N O A I N E U I Y
Rail 3: F T O C

Read off: IMISR NOAINEUI Y FTOC
Ciphertext: IMISRNOAINEUIYTOC

Decryption: Know the pattern, map back to original positions.

Columnar Transposition Cipher

The most important transposition cipher. Write the plaintext in rows under a keyword, then read off the columns in alphabetical key order.

📌 Columnar Transposition — "INFORMATION SECURITY" with key "BEST"
Key:      B  E  S  T
Order:    1  2  4  3    (alphabetical: B=1st, E=2nd, S=4th, T=3rd)

Write plaintext in rows under key (4 columns):
Row 1:    I  N  F  O
Row 2:    R  M  A  T
Row 3:    I  O  N  S
Row 4:    E  C  U  R

⚡
Exam Trick — Transposition vs Substitution

RTU frequently asks: "What is the difference between substitution and transposition cipher?" Perfect 2-mark answer: Substitution = letters are REPLACED (Caesar: A→D, B→E). Letter identity changes. Transposition = letters are REARRANGED (columnar: letters stay same, positions change). Then add: Both together (product cipher) is stronger than either alone — used in DES (S-boxes = substitution, P permutation = transposition).

Transposition Cipher — Definition:
A transposition cipher is a type of classical symmetric encryption technique in which the positions of the characters in the plaintext are rearranged (permuted) without changing the characters themselves. The resulting ciphertext is a permutation of the original plaintext characters.

Key Property: Letters stay the same (A remains A, B remains B), but their positions change. This is fundamentally different from substitution ciphers where letters are replaced with other letters.

Types of Transposition Ciphers:
1. Rail Fence Cipher
2. Columnar Transposition Cipher (most important)
3. Route Cipher

Columnar Transposition — Detailed Example:
Plaintext: "INFORMATION SECURITY" (remove spaces → INFORMATIONSECURITY)

Cryptanalysis

72% · Part A/B

What is Cryptanalysis?

Cryptanalysis is the science of breaking cryptographic systems — analyzing ciphers to recover plaintext or the key without authorized access. Together with cryptography, it forms cryptology.

Cryptography is designing locks. Cryptanalysis is studying how to pick those locks, find their weaknesses, and break them open. Cryptanalysts are the "ethical hackers" of the encryption world — by finding weaknesses, they help build better ciphers.

Connection — Cryptanalysis → Design Principles (Unit 3)

Understanding what cryptanalytic attacks exist directly motivates the design principles in Unit 3! DES has 16 rounds specifically because differential cryptanalysis of 15-round DES is easier than brute force, but full 16-round DES is not. AES was designed to resist all known attacks (differential, linear, interpolation). The S-boxes provide non-linearity to prevent linear cryptanalysis. Knowing the "why" of design principles (Unit 3) requires knowing these attack types from Unit 2.

Brute Force Attack

Try every possible key until the correct one is found. Always succeeds eventually — the question is how long it takes. A 56-bit DES key has 2⁵⁶ ≈ 72 quadrillion possibilities. Modern computers broke DES by brute force in 22 hours (1999). AES-128 with 2¹²⁸ keys is computationally infeasible to brute-force with all computers on Earth combined.

Cryptography: The science of designing methods and algorithms to protect information — creating ciphers, keys, and cryptographic protocols that transform readable data (plaintext) into unreadable form (ciphertext). It focuses on making communication secure. Examples: AES, RSA, SHA-256.

Stream Ciphers vs Block Ciphers

🔥 90% — Every Paper! 🔮 2026 Predicted

Stream Cipher — Bit by Bit

A stream cipher encrypts data one bit (or one byte) at a time by XOR-ing the plaintext with a pseudorandom keystream generated from the key.

A stream cipher is like two people using the same page from a secret random number book. You encrypt each letter by shifting it by the corresponding random number. As long as both sides have the same book and start at the same page, they can decrypt perfectly. The "book" is generated from your shared key.
Stream Cipher Core Formula
Encryption: Cᵢ = Pᵢ XOR Kᵢ (XOR each plaintext bit with keystream bit)
Decryption: Pᵢ = Cᵢ XOR Kᵢ (XOR ciphertext bit with same keystream bit)
Keystream Kᵢ = PRNG(key, nonce) (Pseudorandom Number Generator)
XOR properties: A⊕A=0, A⊕0=A. So encrypting twice with same key = original plaintext. Never reuse a key+nonce!

Examples: RC4 (used in old WEP WiFi), A5/1 (2G GSM voice encryption), ChaCha20 (modern, used in TLS 1.3), one-time pad (theoretically unbreakable)

Block Cipher — Fixed Block Processing

A block cipher encrypts data in fixed-size blocks (typically 64 or 128 bits) through multiple rounds of complex transformations using the same key for each block.

A block cipher is like a meat grinder that takes exactly one pound of meat at a time, puts it through a very complicated grinding mechanism 16 times, and produces one pound of completely different material. To process two pounds, you run it twice.

Examples: DES (64-bit blocks), AES (128-bit blocks), 3DES, Blowfish

Q. Write any two differences between Stream and Block ciphers.
ALL PAPERS 2022–2025 · Part A · 2 marks — 100% Appearance Rate ⭐⭐⭐Stream vs Block🔮 2026 CERTAIN
Difference 1 — Processing Unit:
Stream cipher: encrypts one bit or byte at a time using XOR with a pseudorandom keystream.
Block cipher: encrypts data in fixed-size blocks (DES: 64 bits, AES: 128 bits) all at once through multiple rounds.

Difference 2 — Speed & Application:
Stream cipher: faster due to simple XOR operations; suited for real-time applications (voice calls, streaming video, GSM encryption).
Block cipher: slower but more widely used for secure storage, file encryption, and secure communication protocols (SSL/TLS uses AES).

Additional difference (if 3 asked):
Error propagation: Stream cipher — 1-bit error affects only that bit. Block cipher — 1-bit error may corrupt the entire block (in modes like ECB/CBC).
